# Frailty Trajectories Before and After Incident Cancer in the Health Professionals Follow-up Study

## Project Overview

This project investigates whether developing incident cancer is associated with an accelerated trajectory of frailty compared with remaining cancer-free. The study will use longitudinal data from the Health Professionals Follow-up Study (HPFS), which provides repeated follow-up data over time and allows evaluation of frailty trajectories before and/or after cancer diagnosis.

The project will focus on study design, longitudinal data analysis, missing data methods, and bias assessment. The central analytic challenge is to estimate whether frailty increases more rapidly among participants who develop cancer than among comparable participants who remain cancer-free, while accounting for repeated frailty measurements, missing frailty data, cancer-related mortality, loss to follow-up, and death in older adults.

## Research Question

Does developing incident cancer cause or contribute to an accelerated trajectory of frailty compared with participants who remain cancer-free?

## Primary Hypothesis

Participants who develop incident cancer experience faster increases in frailty over time than comparable participants who remain cancer-free.

## Data Source

The study will use longitudinal data from the Health Professionals Follow-up Study (HPFS). The data include repeated questionnaire-based follow-up and information on health status, cancer incidence, mortality, lifestyle factors, and other covariates relevant to frailty and aging.

## Study Population

The study population will consist of eligible HPFS participants with longitudinal follow-up data and sufficient information to construct a frailty index. Exact inclusion and exclusion criteria will be finalized during the study design phase.

Potential eligibility criteria may include:

* Participants with baseline frailty information available.
* Participants free of cancer at the start of analytic follow-up.
* Participants with repeated frailty index measurements over time.
* Participants with sufficient follow-up to classify incident cancer status.
* Exclusion of participants with prevalent cancer at baseline, depending on the final analytic design.

## Exposure

The main exposure is development of incident cancer during follow-up.

Participants will likely be classified into two groups:

1. Cancer group: participants who develop incident cancer during follow-up.
2. Control group: participants who remain cancer-free during the corresponding follow-up period.

Further work is needed to define the cancer index time and the corresponding reference time for cancer-free controls.

## Outcome

The primary outcome is frailty trajectory over time, measured using a constructed frailty index.

A frailty index has already been constructed in the HPFS data. Cancer is excluded from the frailty index to avoid circularity, since cancer is the main exposure of interest.

The frailty index will be analyzed longitudinally as a repeated outcome. The project will evaluate whether the rate of frailty increase differs between participants who develop cancer and participants who remain cancer-free.

## Key Analytic Objective

The main analytic objective is to estimate and visualize frailty trajectories for participants who develop incident cancer compared with participants who remain cancer-free.

The project will generate trajectory curves comparing frailty over time between the cancer group and the control group. These curves may be aligned by age, calendar time, years since baseline, or years before and after cancer diagnosis, depending on the final study design.

## Primary Longitudinal Modeling Approach: GLME

The first and primary trajectory analysis method will be generalized linear mixed-effects modeling (GLME).

GLME is selected as the initial analytic approach because the study includes repeated frailty index measurements within the same participants. GLME can account for within-person correlation and allow participants to have different baseline frailty levels through random effects.

The primary GLME will estimate whether the frailty trajectory differs between participants who develop incident cancer and participants who remain cancer-free.

A basic model structure may include:

* Frailty index as the repeated longitudinal outcome.
* Time as the longitudinal scale.
* Cancer group indicator.
* Interaction between cancer group and time.
* Random intercept for participant.
* Potential random slope for time, depending on model fit and convergence.
* Adjustment for relevant baseline and/or time-varying covariates.

The key parameter of interest will be the interaction between cancer status and time. This interaction estimates whether the rate of frailty change differs between the cancer group and the cancer-free control group.

Model-based adjusted trajectory curves will be generated to compare frailty progression between groups.

Although GLME will be the first/main modeling approach, additional methods may still be considered later as extensions or sensitivity analyses, especially for addressing informative death, loss to follow-up, and missing frailty data.

## Other Methods to Consider as Secondary or Sensitivity Analyses

While GLME will be the primary starting method, other approaches may be considered depending on the final research question and analytic challenges.

Potential secondary methods include:

* Generalized estimating equations, if the goal is to estimate population-average trajectory differences.
* Piecewise longitudinal models before and after cancer diagnosis.
* Models using time since cancer diagnosis as the time scale.
* Models using age as the time scale.
* Functional data analysis approaches.
* Joint models of longitudinal frailty and survival.
* Inverse probability weighted longitudinal models to address informative dropout or death.
* Survivor average causal effect approaches, if the causal estimand depends on survival.

These methods should be framed as secondary approaches, sensitivity analyses, or methodological extensions rather than the initial primary model.

## Time Alignment and Index Time

A key design decision is how to align time for cancer cases and cancer-free controls.

Possible approaches include:

1. Time since study baseline.
2. Age as the time scale.
3. Calendar year or questionnaire cycle.
4. Time before and after cancer diagnosis.
5. Matched index time for cancer-free controls.
6. Risk-set sampling, where controls are selected from participants still cancer-free at the time when a case develops cancer.

This decision is central because frailty naturally increases with age, and cancer diagnosis occurs at different ages and calendar times across participants.

For the cancer group, the index time may be the date or questionnaire cycle of cancer diagnosis. For the control group, an analogous index time must be assigned carefully to avoid immortal time bias and age-related bias.

## Missing Data

Missing data are expected in frailty index components, covariates, and repeated frailty measurements.

The project will evaluate appropriate imputation methods to handle missing data in the frailty index and relevant covariates.

Potential missing data strategies include:

* Component-level imputation before frailty index construction.
* Frailty-index-level imputation after index construction.
* Multiple imputation for covariates and/or frailty components.
* Longitudinal imputation methods that use repeated measures over time.
* Sensitivity analyses comparing complete-case, partially observed, and imputed results.

The missing data strategy should distinguish among:

* Item-level missingness within frailty components.
* Missing frailty index at a specific questionnaire cycle.
* Missing covariate information.
* Loss to follow-up.
* Death.

Death should not be treated as ordinary missing data because it may be related to both cancer and frailty trajectory.

## Bias and Validity Considerations

Several sources of bias require careful attention.

### Survival Bias and Informative Mortality

Participants who develop cancer may die earlier than participants who remain cancer-free. This can bias frailty trajectory estimates if frailer cancer participants are more likely to die and therefore stop contributing frailty measurements.

Death in general is also a major concern because the study population is aging. Participants who remain alive and continue responding may be systematically healthier than those who die or are lost to follow-up.

Potential approaches include:

* Comparing characteristics of participants with complete versus incomplete follow-up.
* Inverse probability weighting for censoring or dropout.
* Joint modeling of frailty trajectory and mortality.
* Sensitivity analyses restricted to participants who survive a minimum period after index time.
* Analyses that explicitly treat death as a competing or truncating event.
* Survivor average causal effect estimands, if appropriate.

### Loss to Follow-up

Loss to follow-up may be related to health status, frailty, cancer diagnosis, or other factors. If loss to follow-up is informative, standard GLME estimates may be biased.

Potential approaches include inverse probability of censoring weights and sensitivity analyses under different missingness assumptions.

### Confounding

Participants who develop cancer may differ systematically from those who remain cancer-free. Potential confounders may include age, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, baseline frailty, smoking, BMI, physical activity, diet, screening behavior, socioeconomic factors, and other health-related characteristics.

The final adjustment set should be based on a causal framework. If the goal is to estimate the total effect of cancer on frailty progression, the model should avoid adjusting for variables that occur after cancer diagnosis and may lie on the causal pathway.

### Reverse Causation and Preclinical Disease

Frailty may begin to increase before cancer diagnosis due to preclinical disease, cancer-related symptoms, diagnostic workup, or behavioral changes. Therefore, it may be useful to examine whether frailty trajectories diverge before diagnosis, at diagnosis, or only after diagnosis.

Potential analyses include:

* Modeling frailty trajectory several years before and after cancer diagnosis.
* Testing whether frailty slopes differ before diagnosis.
* Excluding frailty measurements close to diagnosis in sensitivity analyses.
* Separating pre-diagnosis and post-diagnosis slope changes.

## Current Working Assumptions

The current working assumptions are:

* Incident cancer is the main exposure.
* Frailty index is the main longitudinal outcome.
* Cancer is excluded from the frailty index.
* The primary comparison is between participants who develop cancer and participants who remain cancer-free.
* GLME will be the first/main trajectory modeling approach.
* The primary model will include a cancer-by-time interaction to estimate differential frailty progression.
* Missing data and survival bias are central methodological concerns.
* Other methods, such as GEE, joint modeling, and inverse probability weighting, may be considered later as secondary or sensitivity analyses.

## Key Methodological Questions for the Project

The project will focus on the following methodological questions:

1. What is the most appropriate time scale for the primary GLME: age, study year, time since baseline, or time relative to cancer diagnosis?
2. How should the cancer-free control group be selected?
3. How should controls be assigned an index time?
4. Should the GLME include random intercepts only, or both random intercepts and random slopes?
5. Should the model use linear, nonlinear, or piecewise time terms?
6. How should missing frailty index data be handled?
7. Should missing frailty components be imputed before constructing the frailty index, or should the final frailty index be imputed?
8. How should death and loss to follow-up be addressed?
9. Should the primary estimand describe frailty trajectories among survivors, or attempt to account for the fact that cancer may increase death and remove frailer participants from observation?
10. Should the analysis explicitly test whether frailty starts accelerating before cancer diagnosis?
11. Should analyses be repeated by cancer type, cancer severity, survival time, or fatal versus nonfatal cancer?
